Materials

Overview

Materials define what your objects look like - whether they appear as metal, plastic, glass, or any other substance. They control color, shininess, and how light interacts with surfaces.

Understanding Materials

If meshes are the shape of your object, materials are its surface appearance. The same sphere geometry can look like a chrome ball bearing, a rubber toy, or a glass marble - entirely depending on its material properties.

Materials in Blender use a physically-based rendering (PBR) system, which means they simulate how light behaves in the real world. This makes creating realistic materials more intuitive, as the parameters correspond to real physical properties.

Key Material Properties

Base Color: The fundamental color of the surface. This is what most people think of as the "color" of an object.

Metallic: Determines whether the surface behaves like metal (value of 1.0) or like a non-metal/dielectric (value of 0.0). Metals reflect their surroundings differently than non-metals. This is typically binary - either 0 or 1, rarely in between.

Roughness: Controls how smooth or rough the surface appears. A value of 0 creates a perfect mirror, while 1.0 creates a completely diffuse, matte surface. Most real materials fall somewhere in between.

Specular: Controls the strength of reflections on non-metallic surfaces. Most non-metals have a specular value around 0.5.

Material Realism: Study real materials and their properties. Plastic is typically non-metallic with medium to high roughness. Polished wood is non-metallic with low roughness. Brushed aluminum is metallic with medium roughness. Understanding these patterns helps you create convincing materials quickly.

Same Shape, Different Materials

In the visualization above, the same sphere geometry is shown with three drastically different materials to demonstrate how materials define appearance:

Left (bronze/brown) - Rough Plastic: High roughness (0.9), non-metallic (0.0), matte brown color. Diffuse, no reflections, absorbs light. This simulates materials like rubber, chalk, or unfinished wood - materials that scatter light in all directions.

Middle (silver) - Polished Metal: Low roughness (0.1), metallic (1.0), silver color. Sharp reflections, shows the environment. Notice how it reflects light differently than non-metals - metals reflect their surroundings tinted by their base color, while non-metals maintain their color and add white specular highlights.

Right (red) - Glossy Plastic: Low roughness (0.2), non-metallic (0.0), bright red color. Sharp but colored reflections. This is typical of glossy paint, polished plastic, or lacquered surfaces - smooth non-metals with clear coatings.

The same exact geometry produces completely different visual results. Materials are what make a sphere look like a billiard ball, a Christmas ornament, or a clay sculpture. Mastering materials means understanding these physical properties and how they combine to create the surfaces you see every day.

Building Complex Materials

Materials can become incredibly complex with texture mapping, normal maps, and node-based shading, but these fundamental properties form the foundation of all material definitions.
