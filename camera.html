Camera

Overview

The camera is your 'eyeball' in the 3D world - it determines what gets shown in your final render. Position it thoughtfully to frame your scene like a photographer!

Understanding the Camera

In a 3D modeling tool, you navigate freely around your 3D scene, viewing it from any angle. But when it comes time to render an image or animation, only one viewpoint matters: the camera's.

Think of it like filmmaking - you can walk around the movie set looking at everything, but the audience only sees what the camera captures. Understanding this distinction is crucial for creating compelling final images.

Camera Controls

The camera is just another object in your scene. You can move and rotate it like any other object, and even animate its position over time. Switch the viewport to look through the camera to see exactly what will be rendered.

Important Camera Properties

Focal Length: Measured in millimeters, this controls the field of view. Lower values (18mm-35mm) create wide-angle shots with more distortion, showing more of the scene. Higher values (85mm-200mm) create telephoto shots with compression, good for portraits. 50mm approximates human vision.

Sensor Size: Combined with focal length, this determines the exact field of view. Default settings (36mm) match full-frame cameras.

Depth of Field: Like real cameras, you can make some areas sharp while others are blurry. Enable it in camera settings, set a focus distance, and adjust the f-stop (lower f-stop = more blur, like f/1.4 for portraits; higher f-stop = more in focus, like f/22 for landscapes).

Composition Principles: Apply photography rules: Rule of thirds (place interesting elements off-center), Leading lines (use geometry to guide the viewer's eye), Headroom (proper spacing above subjects). Most tools can overlay composition guides on the camera view, such as a rule-of-thirds grid or the golden ratio.

Focal Length Comparison

In the visualization below, you see the same scene captured with three different focal lengths:

Left (18mm - Wide Angle): A very wide field of view that shows more of the scene. Notice the slight perspective distortion - objects at the edges appear stretched. Great for architecture, landscapes, and cramped spaces.

Middle (50mm - Normal): Approximates human vision. Natural perspective with minimal distortion. This is the "standard" lens for general-purpose photography.

Right (135mm - Telephoto): A narrow field of view that shows less of the scene but with compressed perspective. The background appears closer to the subject. Perfect for portraits and product shots.

Notice how the object appears similar in size across all three views (the camera position adjusts to compensate), but the background and perspective compression differ dramatically.

Camera Movement in Animation

Static camera: Professional and stable, lets the audience focus on the subject.

Orbiting camera: Dynamic, reveals the three-dimensionality of objects.

Following camera: Tracks moving subjects, common in product visualization and character animation.

Your camera choices affect the emotional impact of your work. Low angles make subjects feel powerful or imposing. High angles make them feel small or vulnerable. Dutch angles (tilted) create unease or dynamism.
