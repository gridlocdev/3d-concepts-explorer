Lighting

Overview

Lights make your 3D scene visible and set the mood - without them everything is pitch black! Different light types create completely different atmospheres.

Understanding Lighting

Lighting can make or break your 3D work. The same model can look photorealistic or amateurish, moody or cheerful, dramatic or flat - entirely based on lighting choices. In many ways, lighting is more important than modeling quality for creating compelling imagery.

Unlike the real world, 3D scenes start in complete darkness. Light doesn't exist unless you explicitly add it. This gives you complete control but also complete responsibility.

Main Light Types in Blender

Point Light: Emits light equally in all directions from a single point, like a bare light bulb. The intensity falls off with distance (inverse square law). Good for local light sources like lamps, candles, or magical effects.

Sun Light: Emits parallel rays in one direction, simulating an infinitely distant light source. The sun's rays are essentially parallel when they reach Earth. Doesn't have distance falloff. Perfect for outdoor scenes.

Spot Light: Emits a cone of light, like a flashlight or theater spotlight. You can control the cone angle and softness. Great for focused, dramatic lighting.

Area Light: Emits light from a rectangular or disk-shaped surface. Creates soft, even lighting similar to photography softboxes or overcast skies. Produces the most natural-looking shadows.

Three-Point Lighting: A classic setup from photography and cinematography: Key light (main light, bright and directional), Fill light (softer light that fills in shadows), and Rim light (from behind, separates subject from background). This setup works for everything from product renders to character portraits.

Three-Point Lighting Setup

In the visualization below, you can see the classic three-point lighting technique in action on a sphere:

Key Light (brightest, top-right): The main light source that defines the primary illumination and creates the dominant shadows. Positioned at about 45 degrees to the subject. This is your "sun" - the light that establishes the scene's overall look.

Fill Light (dimmer, front-left): A softer, less intense light that fills in the shadows created by the key light. It prevents shadows from being pure black and adds depth without flattening the image. Typically 25-50% the intensity of the key light.

Rim/Back Light (behind subject): Positioned behind and to the side of the subject, this light creates a bright edge that separates the subject from the background. It adds depth and makes the subject "pop" visually. Also called a rim light or hair light in portrait photography.

This three-light setup is the foundation of professional lighting in photography, cinematography, and 3D rendering. It creates dimensional, polished results that look professional immediately.

Lighting Considerations

Color temperature: Warm lights (orange/yellow) feel cozy or vintage. Cool lights (blue/white) feel modern or clinical. Different light sources naturally have different colors.

Intensity: Real-world lighting varies enormously in intensity. Don't be afraid to use very bright or very dim lights.

Shadow quality: Soft shadows (from large light sources) feel natural and flattering. Hard shadows (from small/distant sources) create drama and define form.
