Normals

Overview

Normals are invisible arrows pointing out from each face that tell the renderer which side is the 'outside' - get them backwards and your model looks inside-out!

Understanding Normals

Normals are one of those concepts that seem invisible until something goes wrong - and then they become very obvious! Every face on your mesh has an invisible directional vector (think of it as an arrow) pointing perpendicular to its surface.

What normals do: They tell the rendering engine which side of a face is the "outside" and should be visible. This is crucial for how light interacts with your surface and which side actually gets rendered.

Think of a face like a piece of paper - it has two sides, but in 3D graphics, we typically only want to see one of them. The normal determines which side is considered the "front."

Common Problem: If your model appears dark, has black patches, or seems to have holes when it shouldn't, you likely have inverted normals. Select the problematic faces and use your tool's Flip (or Reverse) Normals command to correct them, or have it recalculate all normals to point outward.

What You're Seeing

In the visualization at the start, you'll see two cubes:

Left cube (green): Has correct normals pointing outward. Light bounces off it naturally, and all faces render as expected.

Right cube (orange): Has flipped normals pointing inward. Notice how it appears darker or may look like you're seeing the inside of the cube - because you are! The faces are being rendered from the "back" side.

Smooth vs Flat Shading

In the visualization below, you can see the dramatic effect normal interpolation has on appearance:

Left (orange) - Flat Shading: Each face uses its own normal, perpendicular to that face. This creates hard edges between faces - you can clearly see every polygon. The shading changes abruptly at each edge. Perfect for mechanical objects, faceted gems, or low-poly stylized art.

Right (green) - Smooth Shading: The normals at vertices are averaged between adjacent faces, then interpolated across each face. This creates the illusion of a curved, smooth surface even though the geometry is the same. The shading transitions gradually, making the sphere appear round.

Smooth shading doesn't add geometry - it's purely a lighting trick achieved through normal interpolation. The mesh has the same number of faces either way. This is why smooth shading is so powerful: you get curved-looking surfaces without the performance cost of millions of polygons.

You can combine both techniques: smooth shade most of an object but mark certain edges as "sharp" to preserve hard corners. This is how you model things like a smartphone (smooth rounded sides, but sharp screen edges).

Normals and Shading

Normals also affect smooth shading. When you smooth-shade an object, the renderer interpolates the normals across faces to create the illusion of a curved surface. Understanding normals becomes essential when you want precise control over how light and shadow play across your model.
