UV Maps

Overview

Think of gift-wrapping a box - UV maps unfold your 3D surface into a flat pattern so you can paint textures on it, then wrap them back onto your model.

Understanding UV Mapping

UV mapping solves a fundamental problem: how do you apply a 2D image (like a photograph or painted texture) onto a 3D surface? The answer is to create a correspondence between your 3D mesh and a 2D texture space.

Why "UV"? In 3D space, we use X, Y, and Z coordinates. For 2D texture space, we use U and V to avoid confusion. U corresponds roughly to horizontal (like X), and V to vertical (like Y), but they exist in the flat texture image.

The process works like this:

1. Unwrap: Your 3D model is "unfolded" into a flat pattern, like cutting open a cardboard box and laying it flat. This creates the UV map.

2. Texture: You paint or apply an image to this flat UV layout. Each pixel in the texture corresponds to a specific location on your 3D model.

3. Apply: The renderer wraps the texture back onto the 3D surface using the UV coordinates as a guide.

UV Unwrapping Strategies: Simple objects (cubes, cylinders) can use basic projection unwrapping. Organic objects often need seam-based unwrapping - you mark edges as "seams" where the model can be "cut" and unfolded. Good seam placement (hidden areas, natural breaks) is an art form that affects texture quality.

What You're Seeing

In the visualization at the start, you see a sphere with a grid texture applied. The grid demonstrates how the 2D texture wraps around the 3D surface. Notice:

The U and V labels: These show the coordinate system of the flat texture space - U runs horizontally, V runs vertically.

Grid distortion: The grid squares aren't perfectly uniform on the sphere - they stretch and compress based on how the UV map was created. Minimizing this distortion is a key goal of good UV unwrapping.

Good vs Poor UV Unwrapping

In the visualization below, you can see two cubes with the same checkerboard texture but different UV unwrapping:

Left (white/black) - Good UV Unwrapping: The texture appears uniform across all faces with minimal distortion. Seams are placed at edges where they're less noticeable. The checkerboard squares are consistent in size and shape.

Right (white/black) - Poor UV Unwrapping: Notice the stretched and compressed checkerboard pattern. The texture is distorted because the UV coordinates don't properly account for the 3D surface. Some faces have seams cutting through visible areas.

Good UV unwrapping requires strategic seam placement. Hide seams in areas that won't be seen (undersides, edges, natural breaks in the model). Poor seam placement creates visible distortion and texture misalignment that immediately reveals the model's digital nature.

Why UV Maps Matter

Detailed texturing: Adding specific details like logos, wood grain, or skin pores exactly where you want them.

Texture painting: Painting directly in 2D texture space gives you precise control that's difficult to achieve with procedural textures.

Game assets: Most real-time rendering engines rely heavily on UV-mapped textures for performance reasons.

While procedural textures (generated mathematically) are powerful, UV mapping remains essential for precise, artistic control over your model's appearance.
